## 0. The Model, In One Paragraph

You build small businesses a genuinely good website **on spec, for free**, then cold call/walk in and show the owner their own business already live and looking sharp. That's the hook — nobody says no to seeing something built for them for free. You close a **$500 one-time build fee**, deliver a real, hosted, working site, and keep them on a **$50/month Care Plan** for hosting + small edits + support. If they ever want to walk away with the raw source code and full ownership, that's a separate **$500 buyout fee** — same logic a wedding videographer uses: you pay for the finished, edited video; you pay extra for the raw footage. Most clients never ask for the raw footage. If they cancel the Care Plan without buying out, the site doesn't come down — it just freezes, and any future ask (a small edit or the buyout) is a flat $500 either way, which is what keeps the $50/month the obvious choice. That recurring $50/month, multiplied across dozens of small local clients, is the real business — the $500 builds are what fund your time and prove the work.

## 1. Do This First: Your Own Website

You cannot sell "I'll make you a website that makes you look serious" from a Gmail address and no site of your own. **Before the first cold call, you need your own site live, and it needs to be the best-designed thing in this entire plan** — it's the credibility artifact that makes everything else believable. A prospect's first instinct when a stranger says "I build websites" is to check if you look like someone who's actually good at it.

### What it needs to do
- Prove competence in under 5 seconds of scrolling — this is a portfolio site being judged by people who don't know good design from bad, so it has to *feel* premium even to an untrained eye: generous whitespace, confident typography, real photography, no stock-photo cheese, no lorem ipsum.
- Show, don't tell. Lead with real case studies, not a bullet list of "services."
- Make the $500/$50/$500 offer legible without sounding like a script — a simple, honest pricing section builds trust fast with skeptical small-business owners.
- Convert visitors (and, more realistically, people you already showed something to in person) into a booked call or a "yes, build mine."

### Structure
| Page/Section | Content |
|---|---|
| Home | One sharp headline about what you do for local businesses, 2–3 case study previews, pricing summary, CTA |
| Work / Case Studies | **Kalib Koons** (kalibkoons.com) and **Clementine Speech Therapy** (clementinespeech.com) — full before/after treatment if you can show the original vs. rebuilt site, live links, one line on the result for each |
| Process | The 3-step version of what you do: free mockup → $500 live build → optional $50/mo care |
| Pricing | $500 build / $50/mo care / $500 buyout — laid out simply, no hidden fees language |
| About | You — why local, why St. Pete, why small businesses specifically (this is a trust play, not a resume) |
| Contact | Simple form (reuse Web3Forms, same as Clementine) + phone/email |

### Design & tech direction
- Reuse the proven formula from the Kalib project: **plain HTML/CSS/JS, no framework, no build step**, hosted on **GitHub Pages** with a custom domain via **Namecheap**, contact form via **Web3Forms**. It's fast, cheap, and — critically — it means every client site you show off was built the same lean way you'd build theirs, which is a credibility asset, not a limitation.
- This site should be visually distinct from both Kalib's (dark/gold/authoritative) and Clementine's (warm/botanical/calm) — you need your own identity. Once you land on a business name (Section 2), the palette and type should flow from that name the same way "Clementine" drove the orange/citrus palette.
- Get a real headshot or at least a clean personal photo — an "about the founder" section with no photo reads as anonymous and hurts trust with older, in-person small-business owners.
- Before/after sliders or side-by-side screenshots of the old vs. new Kalib Koons site (if you still have the old version archived) would be a genuinely strong conversion element — this is your clearest "here's what I do" proof.

### When to build it
Build this **before you approach any of the 5 target businesses in Section 3.** It doesn't need to be finished before you start *drafting* their rough sites (that work can run in parallel), but it needs to be live before the first phone call or walk-in — you'll want to be able to say "check out [yourbusinessname].com" and have it hold up.

## 4. Business Model & Pricing

### The three-tier structure (wedding videographer model)

| Tier | Price | What they get | Analogy |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The Build** | **$500 one-time** | A live, professionally designed, hosted website — the finished, edited product | The edited 2-minute highlight video |
| **Care Plan** | **$50/month, ongoing** | You host it, keep it running, make small edits/updates on request — **and if they ever want it, you'll redo the entire site from scratch, your creative call** | The videographer's optional archive/backup service |
| **Buyout** | **$500 one-time, optional, anytime** | Full source code + assets handed over, they own everything outright, can host it anywhere, cancels the need for the Care Plan | Paying extra for the raw, unedited footage |

### The economics
- $500 build is priced to be an easy yes for a small business owner — roughly one good day of revenue for most of your target categories, not a scary business-loan-sized decision.
- $50/month is the real business. Ten active Care Plan clients = $500/month in recurring revenue for maintenance work that should take you under an hour total, if the sites are built cleanly and the requests are genuinely small.
- The buyout exists for two reasons: (1) it's a second revenue event from the same client if they ever want out, and (2) **its existence is what makes the $50/month feel optional and fair rather than a lock-in** — nobody feels trapped if there's a clearly priced exit.
- **Lapsed Care Plan default, stated plainly in the contract:** if a client cancels, the site does **not** come down — it stays live exactly as it was, frozen/static (see Section 8). Because it's a static site on GitHub Pages with the client owning their own domain (Section 8, item 7), leaving it live costs you nothing, so there's no reason to threaten a takedown — it just sits there as a standing reminder of the work.
- **The catch that makes this work:** once they're off the plan, **any further work — a single small edit or a full redesign — costs the same as their original Build fee**, treated exactly like a brand-new build engagement (unscoped, your creative call, full redesign if they want one) — the only difference from a first-time client is there's already a live site to start from instead of a blank page. That same number is also the buyout price, so it doubles as either (a) full source code handed over, they own it outright, no more relationship needed, or (b) a fresh round of work on the site as you continue hosting it, after which it goes frozen again. **One rule covers both contract tracks:** whatever a client paid to build their site — $500 flat under Contract A, or $1,000–1,500 under Contract B (Section 4b) — is the fixed number locked into their agreement for both buyout and reactivation, so it's never a separate number you have to remember or re-negotiate. It doesn't rise with future new-client pricing; it's set once, at their original Build.
- **The cycle repeats on purpose.** Resubscribing to the Care Plan after paying that reactivation fee is always optional, never required — but if they decline it, the site just re-freezes, and the *next* time they come back wanting anything at all, it's the same fee again. That's stated plainly enough up front that it's clearly on them, not a surprise. Paying that fee repeatedly for one-off touches versus staying on the monthly plan for the same (and the included redesign perk on top) is exactly the math that makes staying on the plan the obviously better deal.

### What counts as "small" under the Care Plan — three tiers
Be specific in your own head (and eventually in the contract) about what's included, so you're never negotiating scope live on the phone. Lay this out as three tiers, not a binary included/not-included list — it gives you room to say yes to something bigger without either promising it's free or having to quote a price on the spot:

1. **Included, unlimited, fast turnaround (same day/next day):** text/copy changes, swapping photos, updating hours/menu/pricing, adding a new testimonial, fixing a broken link, minor color/layout tweaks, adding a single new service listing. Rule of thumb for yourself: if it takes you under 20–30 minutes, it's this tier.
2. **Included, uncapped, your creative call:** a full ground-up redesign of the existing site — see "The full-redesign perk" below. This covers *re-imagining what's already there*, not adding capabilities the site doesn't have.
3. **"Other" — discussed case-by-case on a quick call:** anything that doesn't obviously fit tiers 1 or 2 — e-commerce/online ordering, a booking system integration, net-new photography/videography you'd need to go shoot, a genuinely new feature (a blog, a members area, a multi-location expansion, a third-party integration). Default response to any of these: **"let's hop on a quick call so I understand exactly what you're picturing — that kind of thing might come with a little extra cost, or take an extra month or two depending on what's involved, but let's talk through it."** This keeps you from having to give an instant yes/no/price over text, and sets the expectation up front that "bigger than normal" can mean both added cost *and* added time, without you needing a fixed price list for every edge case.

### The full-redesign perk (the real hook of the Care Plan)
This is the headline of the $50/month offer, not a footnote: **while they're an active Care Plan client, they can ask you to rebuild their site from scratch, and it's included.** Not a redesign *of their spec* — a redesign at *your* creative discretion. That distinction is what keeps this sane on your end:
- **They're asking, you're deciding.** The pitch is "full creative freedom to redo it" meaning *your* creative freedom, not an open brief they get to art-direct round after round. One reinterpretation, delivered as a finished thing — same "show, don't ask them to imagine it" model you already use for the free mockup and the reveal meeting. If they don't like the direction, that's a conversation, not an obligation to keep iterating indefinitely.
- **No cap, by design.** Realistically almost no one asks for this more than once, if ever — most owners are relieved just having a good site, not chasing rebrands. The offer is intentionally stated as unlimited because that's what makes it a genuine headline instead of a hedge; if a specific client ever *does* start invoking it often, that's a one-on-one conversation about pace/turnaround, not a rule you need written down in advance.
- **It's still bound by "active Care Plan."** The moment they cancel or take the $500 buyout, this perk (like hosting itself) goes with it — reinforce that in the pitch so it never sounds like a permanent, plan-independent entitlement.
- **Free/included covers your normal pace, not any pace.** The redesign itself is included, no strings — but if someone wants it rushed on a specific deadline ("I need it in a week"), or wants new capabilities bundled into the redesign (a booking system, e-commerce, anything that isn't just re-imagining the existing site), that's the Tier 3 "Other" conversation from the section above, not an automatic part of the free perk. Keep the pitch itself unconditional-sounding ("full creative freedom to redo it, included") — the rush/scope-expansion carve-out only needs to come up if and when someone actually asks for it, not as a hedge stated up front.
- **Why it's worth it even in the rare case someone uses it:** $50/month is $600/year in recurring revenue from a single client who was otherwise a one-time $500 sale. Even a client who cashes in a full rebuild once a year is still a client generating $600/year of relationship value, on top of the retention and referral value of being the guy who "just handles it." The economics only break if it becomes the norm rather than the exception — which is exactly why this stays a spoken selling point, not a scoped SOW.

## 4b. Pro Care Plan — A Second Contract Track for Higher-Value Clients (Draft, Not Yet Live)

**Status: drafted for review, not reflected on clementinewebco.com yet.** The $500/$50/$500 model in Section 4 stays exactly as-is for the barbershop/food-truck/salon segment — don't touch that pricing, the "less than a dinner out" anchor is deliberately tuned to cash-tight operators. This section adds a **second contract track**, not a "higher tier of the same thing," for a different kind of client: local professional-service businesses where a single new customer is worth far more than a haircut.

### Where this came from
An informal audit of a friend's practice site, **Eagle Sport & Spine** (eaglesportandspine.com, a St. Pete sports chiropractor), triggered this. He's paying **$275/month** for "hosting + SEO" to a freelancer. The site itself:
- Loads in **10–15 seconds** (Google's "good" threshold is 2.5s) — a severe, measurable problem, not a matter of taste.
- Has **no Google Analytics, Tag Manager, or Meta Pixel** — no conversion tracking of any kind.
- Has **no phone number anywhere on the homepage**, and no `tel:` link.
- Has **no LocalBusiness/MedicalBusiness schema** (only a bare Organization block — missing phone, address, hours, geo).
- Runs on a **duplicate live domain** (masteringmotion.com mirrors the same business with no canonical/redirect), splitting whatever search authority it has.
- Shows no real Google Business Profile / review presence in search — the single biggest local-pack ranking factor, apparently unmanaged.

What *is* done well: 22 dedicated service pages each targeting a specific long-tail local search term, a well-formed title/meta description, and genuinely good positioning copy. So the content strategy has real thought behind it — the technical execution (the part that actually requires ongoing, skilled work) is where it falls apart. **The takeaway isn't "he's being scammed," it's "this is the market rate for mediocre execution."** A client base like this will pay $200–300+/month even when what they're getting is this thin, because the math is on their side: one retained patient is worth thousands over time, so $275/month is a rounding error, not a decision.

### Naming — avoid "growth," avoid ranked-sounding labels
Nothing about this needs to sound like a hierarchy, and none of it is ever displayed publicly side-by-side (Section 4 already moved Care Plan pricing off the site entirely — it's quoted live, per client). So the names only need to work for you, internally, and in two separate signed agreements:
- **Care Plan** — the existing $50/month plan, unchanged, no rename needed.
- **Pro Care Plan** — the new one. "Pro" describes the client's business (a licensed professional practice), not a ranking of the client themselves. Avoids "growth," "premium," "tier 2," or anything that implies one client is more valued than another.

### The real differentiation — it's not affordability, it's scope of ongoing work
This is the important distinction: **Pro Care Plan clients aren't paying more for the same service because they can afford it — they're paying for a genuinely different, larger scope of ongoing work.** If the only difference were the price, this would be pure price discrimination and hard to defend if two clients ever compared notes. Instead:

| | **Care Plan** ($50/mo) | **Pro Care Plan** (new) |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | Reactive maintenance | Reactive maintenance **+** proactive marketing management |
| Hosting, unlimited small edits, full-redesign perk | Included | Included |
| Analytics + call tracking (GA4/GTM installed, monitored) | Not included | Included |
| LocalBusiness/MedicalBusiness schema, kept current | Not included | Included |
| Google Business Profile management + review-generation system | Not included | Included |
| Ongoing page-speed/technical health checks | Not included | Included |
| Monthly written check-in (what changed, what's next) | Not included | Included |
| Response time | Standard | Priority |

That's the honest answer to "what's the real difference in value": Care Plan keeps a site online and current. Pro Care Plan actively works the site as a marketing asset every month. That's real, ongoing labor on your end — the price difference maps to real scope, not just what the client can afford.

### The build itself — what actually makes it bigger, not just pricier
Same principle applies to the Build fee as to the Care Plan above. If a client ever asks "why is my build a thousand dollars when [barbershop] paid $500," this is the real, defensible answer — not "because you can afford it":

| | **Contract A build** ($500) | **Contract B build** ($1,000–1,500) |
|---|---|---|
| Page count | One page (or a few sections on one page) | 8–15 real pages — a dedicated page per condition/service treated |
| Content depth | Hours, services/menu, gallery, a few testimonials — brief, scannable | Credentials, methodology, insurance/payment info, a real FAQ — written to build trust before first contact |
| Lead capture | Simple contact form | Tracked, structured intake/consultation flow, built in from day one |
| Technical setup at launch | Basic on-page SEO for one location/service | LocalBusiness/MedicalBusiness schema, analytics, call tracking wired in before launch, not retrofitted later |
| Multi-provider support | Not applicable | Individual bio pages, if more than one practitioner |
| Revision rounds | Straightforward — "does this look right" | More back-and-forth — medical/legal claims have to be worded carefully |

**Who's on which side, concretely:**
- **Contract A:** Barber Sami, Tyrone Barber Shop, Ubuntu Food Truck, Alphonse Grooming, Nails by Lina (Section 3) — same shape as house cleaners, lawn care, dog walkers, coffee shops, solo personal trainers. Single location, single service category, fast/low-stakes buying decision — "does this look legit, what are the hours, can I see their work."
- **Contract B:** chiropractors, dentists, PTs, med spas, law firms, vets, home-service contractors (HVAC/plumbing/electrical/roofing) — Eagle Sport & Spine (above) is the direct comp. Buying decision is slow, researched, high-stakes — before someone calls a chiropractor or hires a contractor for a $10,000 roof, they read credentials, compare providers, want to feel informed. A one-page site can't carry that; it takes real page count and content depth to earn the call.

**Why this matters more than the ongoing-scope table above:** the Care Plan difference only comes up after someone's already a client. The Build difference is what a prospect sees and evaluates *before* paying anything — it has to visibly justify itself standing alone, not just ride along with the monthly plan.

**The one-line answer, if a client asks directly:** not *"because you can afford more"* — it's *"your site does more work, and stays actively managed as a marketing asset, not just kept online."* Both the build (real page count, real content depth, lead infrastructure) and the ongoing plan (analytics, schema upkeep, GBP/review management, monthly check-ins vs. reactive small edits) are genuinely bigger in scope, not just pricier for the same thing.

|---|---|---|---|

A Contract A client's buyout/reactivation is $500; a Contract B client's is whatever they paid within the $1,000–1,500 range, fixed at signing.

### Market comparison — why these numbers aren't a stretch
Pulled via web search, August 19, 2026 — verify current rates before quoting, this market moves:
- **Small-business website builds:** freelancers typically charge $500–5,000, agencies $3,000–15,000+, with most professional small-business builds landing $3,000–8,000. A $1,000–1,500 Build fee for Contract B is on the affordable end of that range, not a stretch for a licensed professional practice already carrying equipment/insurance/lease costs.

The actual gap is execution quality (missing analytics, schema, page speed) on a platform whose plugins make those things easy to get right. Worth noting as a genuine differentiator: Clementine's hand-coded, no-framework approach (Section 1) is what the market normally reserves for $20k+ custom builds — "leaner, faster, more secure, built exactly for the goal" — a legitimate selling point, not just an internal cost-saving choice.

## 5. Marketing the Value of the $50/Month

The single biggest risk to this model is a client asking "why would I pay $50/month for a website that's already done?" You need language ready that never lets the conversation become about hosting cost (which is commoditized and cheap-sounding) and instead keeps it about **their time and risk**.

### Framing principles
1. **Never call it "hosting."** Hosting sounds like a $5/month commodity they could get anywhere. Call it the **Care Plan**, or "keeping the site running and current."
2. **Sell the absence of their own labor.** They don't want to learn Wix or Squarespace, log in, find the right block, and fumble through an edit themselves. You're the person they text a photo to.
3. **Anchor against the real alternative cost.** A freelancer charges $75–150/hour for small changes — a $50/month plan that covers unlimited small requests is cheap insurance against ever needing to find and pay someone last-minute.
4. **Tie it to outcomes that cost them money if neglected.** An outdated menu, old hours, or a broken contact form actively loses them customers — that's the real cost of *not* having the plan, not the $50 itself.
5. **Make it personal and local.** "I'm a phone call away, I'm in St. Pete, I'm not a call center" is a genuine differentiator against Wix/Squarespace subscriptions and overseas freelancers.
6. **Lead with the ceiling, not just the floor.** Don't just sell "small edits included" — that undersells it and invites the "why pay monthly for a finished thing" objection. Sell the fact that the plan covers everything from a one-line text fix up to a full ground-up redesign, at your creative discretion, whenever they want it. Most people will never use the top end of that — which is exactly what makes it safe to say out loud.

### Ready-to-use phrasing
> "The $500 gets you live. The $50 a month just means you never have to touch it again — if your hours change, you get a new photo, you want to swap out a promotion, you just text me and it's done, usually same day. Think of it like having a website guy on retainer for less than a dinner out."

> "Most of my clients don't ever think about their website once it's live — that's the point. The $50 a month is what makes it stay that way instead of going stale, which is what happens to almost every small business site within a year."

> "And it's not just small stuff — if a year from now you want the whole thing reinvented, new look, new layout, whatever, that's included too. You tell me you want a change, I take full creative freedom on it and just redo it. Most people never need that, but it's there."

## 6. Sales Process — End to End

```
1. RESEARCH        → Confirm business is open, pull name/address/photos
2. BUILD MOCKUP    → Free rough-draft site, on spec, no contact yet
3. TEXT/EMAIL → CALL  → Send the link first, no interruption; call 2-3 days later only if no response
4. REVEAL MEETING  → Show the site, close the $500 build, take payment + signature on the spot
5. ASSET REQUEST   → Hand them the 5-item checklist (Section 7b-ii); they send it via text/email, no rush
6. BUILD           → Rebuild the real site with their real content once assets arrive
7. REVIEW CALL     → Scheduled 30-min call, 2-3 days out: walk through the finished site, take edits live, present Care Plan vs. Buyout
8. GO LIVE         → Publish on their domain, same day as the review call
9. ONGOING CARE    → Monthly billing, respond to small-edit requests fast
```

The reason this works better than a typical cold-call pitch: **you're never asking them to imagine anything.** By step 3 you already have a finished, real-looking website with their actual business name on it. That collapses most of the normal sales cycle — the hardest part of selling a website (getting the prospect to picture the outcome) is already done before you ever speak to them.

**Why steps 5–7 are split out explicitly:** a lot of these owners aren't going to sit down and think through "what does my new website need" on their own — that's an open-ended task most people procrastinate on indefinitely. A short, numbered, "I need exactly these 5 things from you" list is something they can knock out in five minutes between customers. And scheduling the review call up front (instead of a vague "I'll text you when it's ready") gives them a fixed, small commitment on their calendar instead of an open loop they have to remember to follow up on.

### 7a-ii. Cold Call Script (Phone-Only, No Text/Email or Owner Name On File)

Use this when a prospect genuinely has no other channel — no owner name, no email, and the phone number itself came from a directory listing rather than the business's own site. The call *is* the introduction. Same goal as 7a: get permission to text the link, nothing more — don't try to describe the website in detail over the phone, and don't try to close anything on a cold call.

**Opening:**
> "Hi, is this [Business Name]? ... Hey, my name's Devin, I run a small web design studio here in [City], Clementine Web Co. Do you have a quick second?"

**If yes:**
> "Great — I came across [Business Name] on [Angi/Yelp/wherever found], saw the reviews, and noticed there wasn't really a website to go with it. So I actually went ahead and built a free sample site, no charge, just to show what it could look like. Would it be alright if I text you the link so you can take a look whenever you get a chance?"

**If they ask why you'd do that for free:**
> "Fair question — it's basically how I find new clients. I build the sample first so you can see exactly what you'd be getting before anyone pays anything. If you like it, I can build out the real thing for a flat $500. If not, no hard feelings, it cost you nothing to look."

**If they seem busy or rushed:**
> "No worries, I'll keep it quick — can I just text you the link and you can look whenever's convenient? Takes two seconds."

**If they say they're not interested before you even get the ask out:**
> "Totally understand — mind if I just leave the link with you in case that changes? No follow-up calls, I promise."

**Voicemail / no answer:** Don't leave a long cold voicemail, they rarely get returned. Built a free sample website for [Business Name], no charge, would love for you to take a look. Give me a call back at [number], or I'll try again another time." **Before you dial — verify the lead is actually worth the call:** a phone-only, no-name prospect usually means the research was thinner than usual, so sanity-check it's still open first: search the business name plus "closed," check Sunbiz.org for LLC status if it's a Florida LLC (a voluntary or administrative dissolution filed years ago, combined with no recent reviews, is a strong sign it's no longer operating even if directory listings still show it), and check the actual dates on reviews, not just the star rating — a 5.0 rating from reviews that are all 5+ years old with nothing since means the listing is stale, not that the business is thriving. ## 8. Contracts & Paperwork

You need a short, plain-English **Website Development & Care Plan Agreement** before you take money from a real client — verbal terms on ownership and cancellation *will* eventually cause a dispute. At minimum it should cover:

1. **Scope** — what's included in the $500 build (number of pages, one round of revisions, etc.)
2. **Payment terms** — $500 due before work begins, or 50% deposit / 50% on delivery (recommend full amount up front given the low price point and small-business context — reduces your collection risk)
3. **Ownership & IP** — explicitly state that **you retain ownership of the source code and design** unless the client pays the $500 buyout; the client is licensed to use the live, hosted site as long as they're current on the Care Plan
4. **Care Plan terms** — $50/month, the three-tier scope from Section 4 (unlimited small edits / included full-redesign perk / "Other" requests scoped case-by-case and priced or timelined separately if warranted), response time expectation (e.g., "within 2 business days"), cancel-anytime language — no guaranteed specific turnaround time stated in writing for the redesign perk or "Other" requests (agree those verbally/case-by-case so you're never contractually on the hook for a rebuild or a new feature by a fixed date)
5. **Cancellation / reactivation policy** — explicitly state the site does **not** come down on cancellation; it goes static and frozen (no further edits) until they either pay the $500 buyout, or pay a flat $500 for a fresh, unscoped round of work on the site (treated like a brand-new build engagement, up to and including a full redesign — site freezes again afterward unless they resubscribe), or resubscribe to the $50/month Care Plan outright (fully optional, never required). Make clear in the contract that this $500-per-return cycle repeats indefinitely each time they come back without resubscribing.
6. **Buyout terms** — $500 flat fee, what's handed over (full source, any purchased assets, domain transfer help), timeline
7. **Domain ownership** — two cases:
   - **Client already has a domain they want to keep:** they keep managing/owning it as-is, no change needed.
   - **Client has no domain yet:** you register it for them (no signup friction on their end) under your Namecheap account, **but the WHOIS/registrant contact is set to their business info, not yours** — you manage it operationally, they're the legal registrant of record. This is what actually prevents the "ugly disputes" risk, not who clicks renew.
   - **Cost handling:** year one is a rounding error, absorbed into the $500 build. Ongoing years, domain renewal (~$12–15/yr) is bundled into the $50/month Care Plan — call it out explicitly in the pitch (Section 5) as a second real recurring cost you're covering, not just "your time." If a client lapses (no Care Plan, no buyout), you don't auto-renew out of pocket and you don't run a formal transfer process — near the renewal date, send a one-line heads-up offering to renew it for the ~$15 fee or hand the account over to them; if there's no response, it lapses naturally through the registrar. Same non-punitive logic as the site itself going frozen instead of taken down (Section 4, Section 8 item 5) — it just quietly stops being your cost the moment they're not paying for it, without you having to do anything confrontational.
8. **Liability/disclaimer** — standard limitation of liability, no guarantee of business results from the site

You can draft a first version of this yourself in a Google Doc, or ask me to draft the actual agreement text once you're ready — it's a natural next step once the entity is registered.

**E-signature:** use a free tier of HelloSign/Dropbox Sign or DocuSign so clients can sign on the spot from their phone at the reveal meeting.

### Payments & billing — fully remote, no physical hardware, fees minimized

Every dollar moves because a client taps a link on their own phone, tablet, or computer — never a card reader, never anything you carry or plug in. One processor (**Stripe**) handles all of it: the one-time build fee, the recurring Care Plan, saved payment methods, and client self-service.

**The $500 build fee (one-time):**
- A Stripe Payment Link for the $500 build fee, with "save payment method for future use" turned on.
- At the reveal meeting close (Section 7b), text or email the link on the spot — the client pays from whatever device is in their hand, whether that's while you're sitting there or after you've left.
- Because the payment method is saved as part of that first checkout, it's already on file the moment they've paid — nothing extra to set up before the Care Plan starts.

**The $50/month Care Plan (recurring):**
- At the review call (Section 7c), once they agree to the Care Plan, start a Stripe subscription against the payment method already saved from the $500 checkout — no second card entry, no separate link, if they paid the build fee digitally.
- If there's no saved method on file for some reason, send a second Stripe Checkout link in subscription mode — same one-tap, own-device flow.

**Card/bank management, cancellation, reactivation:**
- Every Care Plan client gets a link to Stripe's hosted **Customer Portal**, where they can update an expired card, swap bank accounts, see billing history, or cancel the plan themselves — matches the "cancel anytime" language already in the scripts, and means you're not the one manually processing every card update.
- A lapsed client coming back for the flat $500 reactivation fee (Section 4) gets the same Payment Link pattern — a returning customer's saved method is often recognized instantly via Stripe's fast-checkout, otherwise it's a fresh 30-second checkout.

**Reducing Stripe's cut — default to ACH, not cards:**
- Enable both **card** and **US bank account (ACH Direct Debit)** as payment options in every Stripe Link/Checkout, and put ACH first in the payment method order so it's the path of least resistance rather than an obscure option.
- The math is real money over time: a card charges ~2.9% + $0.30 — about **$14.80** on the $500 build and **$1.75** every month on the Care Plan. ACH is ~0.8%, capped at $5 — about **$4.00** on the $500 build and **$0.40** a month on the Care Plan. Across a few dozen Care Plan clients over a few years, that gap compounds into real dollars.
- Trade-off worth knowing: ACH takes a few business days to settle (vs. near-instant for cards) and has a small chance of a delayed return (insufficient funds, wrong account) that shows up after the payment initially looks successful. Low-stakes at these transaction sizes, but don't rely on an ACH payment as "cleared" the instant it's initiated.
- Skip Stripe's **Instant Payouts** (~1% extra) — standard payouts (1–2 business days) are free and there's no real urgency to get paid same-day.
- A clean, well-scoped contract (Section 8) does double duty on fees: fewer disputed/refunded charges, and Stripe doesn't return its processing fee on a refund even when you do.

| Level | What | When | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Federal | Estimated income + self-employment tax (Form 1040-ES) | Quarterly — mid-April, mid-June, mid-September, mid-January | A single-member LLC is a default "disregarded entity" — profit flows to your personal 1040 and owes both ordinary income tax and ~15.3% self-employment tax (Social Security + Medicare). A common rule of thumb is setting aside ~25–30% of net profit, but confirm your actual rate with a CPA rather than relying on a rule of thumb once money is real. |
| Federal | Schedule C (attached to your personal Form 1040) | Annually, standard tax deadline (~April 15) | Reports the LLC's profit/loss as part of your personal return. |
| State (FL) | Sunbiz Annual Report | Every year by **May 1** | **$138.75**; a $400 late fee applies automatically with zero grace period (Section 9, item 12) — put a calendar reminder in now. |
| State (FL) | Sales tax | Confirm, don't assume | Florida generally doesn't tax professional/service work like web design (unlike tangible goods), so a sales tax permit is likely not required — but confirm this directly with a CPA or the FL Department of Revenue before assuming it doesn't apply, since misclassifying a taxable service carries real penalty risk. |
| State (FL) | Personal income tax | None | Florida has no state personal income tax — one less filing to track. |
| Local (St. Pete) | Business Tax Receipt renewal | Annually (confirm exact renewal date with the city — commonly around Sept 30 for FL municipalities) | One-time application above, then a recurring renewal. |

**Housekeeping, not government-mandated but worth doing:** keep the Operating Agreement, EIN letter, Articles of Organization, and bank documents together in one place; renew the domain and Microsoft 365 subscription before they lapse; and once revenue is consistently strong enough to matter, ask a CPA about an S-corp election — a potential self-employment tax saver at higher profit levels, but added payroll complexity isn't worth it this early.
